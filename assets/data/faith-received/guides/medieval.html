From Abelard to Gabriel Biel (1120–1495)

The schools of the Latin Middle Ages: the masters of Paris and Oxford who lectured on the Sentences and wrote the summas, the friars who argued Aristotle into theology, and the canonists of Bologna who built the law of the Church. The texts are in Latin with English beside them.

1 · Where they taught

The masters on the road

A master of the schools was seldom at home. Thomas Aquinas taught twice at Paris and also at Cologne, Orvieto, Rome and Naples. Ockham went from Oxford to the pope’s court at Avignon and died in Munich.

    2 · The sentences

    Sayings set side by side

    A sentence (sententia) was a saying with authority behind it, most often from the Fathers. Abelard’s Sic et Non set sayings that seemed to disagree side by side. Gratian did the same for the canons of the Church. Peter Lombard gathered the sentences at Paris about 1158 in four books that run from God to the last things. He judged between them.

    His book became the textbook. Every bachelor of theology lectured on it before he could become a master. Lombard’s text is on the Latin Fathers shelf in volume 192 of Migne. This shelf holds the commentaries.

    3 · The disputed question

    How a question is argued

    The disputation came with Aristotle. Once his logic and natural philosophy were read in the schools, the masters put each matter as a question and argued it to a conclusion. The Summa of Alexander of Hales was begun at Paris in the 1230s and is built this way. So are Albert’s and Thomas’s summas and Bonaventure’s commentary on the Sentences.

      4 · The law

      Text and gloss

      At Bologna the law of the Church was taught the way theology was taught at Paris. The text stood in the middle of the page. The masters’ commentary was written around it. The shelf holds the whole Corpus of Canon Law from Gratian to the Extravagantes. It also holds the great commentaries on it.

        5 · Aristotle

        Aristotle in the schools

        From the middle of the thirteenth century the friars commented on Aristotle book by book. Albert paraphrased nearly the whole of him. Thomas expounded him line by line. Scotus and his followers asked questions of him. Each circle is a commentary on the shelf. Its size shows its pages.
