The Continental Reformed shelf

From Zwingli’s Zurich to the Huguenot refuge (1520–1760)

The churches of Zurich, Geneva, the Palatinate, the Netherlands and France built their own schools. This shelf holds Calvin and Bullinger, the professors of Heidelberg, Leiden, Utrecht and Saumur, and the great systems of Reformed orthodoxy. The texts are in Latin and sometimes French. English stands beside every page.

1 · What is on the shelf

Nine kinds of book

Four hundred and thirty-one works by a hundred and ten writers run from Zwingli’s Zurich to the Dutch academies of the eighteenth century. Here they are sorted by kind, as a printer sorted type into the compartments of a case.

    2 · The generations

    Two hundred years of Reformed schools

    The width of each stream is the number of the shelf’s writers at work in those years. Each stream is a region where they taught. The weight moved from the Swiss cities to the German academies and on to the Dutch universities. The French schools ended with the Revocation.

    3 · The Reformed International

    A church of refugees and professors

    The Reformed churches had no single capital. Their teachers fled or were called and moved on. Junius went from Bourges to Geneva, Heidelberg and Leiden. Alsted went from Herborn to Transylvania. The map marks each academy in the year it opened and follows the writers between them.

      4 · What the churches confessed

      A confession for every church

      The Lutherans gathered their confessions into one Book of Concord. Each Reformed city, kingdom, synod and refugee congregation wrote its own. The library holds 127 of them in its Confessions room. Each small card is one confession. The major confessions appear as full cards.

      5 · Zurich, Geneva, Heidelberg, Leiden

      The Bible, book by book

      The Reformed began with the Bible. These are the commentaries and homilies on the shelf that work through a book of Scripture: Each row is a writer and each column a book. The writers stand in the order they worked.

        6 · Heidelberg

        One hundred and twenty-nine questions

        The Heidelberg Catechism of 1563 was taught in the Palatinate, the Netherlands and far beyond. It was divided into fifty-two Lord’s Days and taught Sunday by Sunday. Three commentaries on the shelf take it question by question. Each bar is the pages one of them gives a question, as the printed book heads it.

          7 · Dort

          Five articles, and the synod’s answer

          In 1610 the followers of Arminius set out their teaching in five articles, the Remonstrance. The States General called a national synod to Dordrecht. Theologians came from Britain, the Palatinate, Hesse, the Swiss cities, Geneva, Bremen and Emden. It answered the five articles under four heads of doctrine. The third and fourth articles share one head. Each head closes with the errors it rejected.

              8 · The method

              Divide and divide again

              Peter Ramus taught that any subject is best laid out by dividing it in two and then each part in two again. The Reformed schools took to it. Amandus Polanus of Basel built his Syntagma of Christian Theology (1609) this way. Its first two books open with the whole tree. The tree runs from theology to the two tables of the law. Choose a branch to open its chapter.
