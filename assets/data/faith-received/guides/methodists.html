The Methodists

From the Holy Club at Oxford to the Methodist Episcopal Church

The Wesleys and George Whitefield, the preachers who rode their circuits, and the American church that grew out of them: sermons and hymns, journals and letters, three commentaries on the whole Bible, and a long argument about grace. The books were printed in London, Bristol, Boston and Philadelphia from 1737 to the end of the nineteenth century.

1 · An Atlantic revival

The ocean between

The revival crossed the Atlantic from its first years. The Wesleys went out to Georgia in 1735. Whitefield crossed thirteen times and died in Massachusetts. Asbury sailed in 1771 and never came home. Their books were printed wherever they preached.

    2 · The argument

    Free grace

    In 1739 Wesley preached at Bristol against the doctrine that God has chosen some and passed by the rest. Whitefield answered him. For a century Methodists argued it out with Calvinists in England and New England. The shelf holds both sides of the first quarrel. The later opponents appear here by name.

    3 · The hymnbook

    A body of divinity in hymns

    In his preface of 1779 Wesley called the Collection of Hymns for the People Called Methodists “a little body of experimental and practical divinity”. It is ordered as a life. The sinner is exhorted and convinced and repents. The believer rejoices, fights, prays, watches, works, suffers and seeks full redemption. The society meets and parts. The edition on the shelf adds a supplement.

    4 · Scripture

    One Bible, four commentaries

    Wesley’s Explanatory Notes and the three Methodist commentaries on the whole Bible that followed it: Coke’s, Benson’s and Clarke’s. They run book by book from Genesis to Revelation. Each bar is one book in one commentary. Its height is its number of pages. Choose a bar to read it. An empty place is a book these volumes lack, such as Clarke’s Romans.
